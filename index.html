What does this assessment estimate?

The population and its model prediction advance every year, using reported catch and Beverton–Holt recruitment. At a scheduled assessment, the model processes survey data collected since the previous assessment and updates the stock estimate. Between assessments, surveys are stored for the next fit and catch advice stays fixed.

It reports estimated numbers at age, SSB, and the fishing rate needed to explain the previous catch. Biological rates and q are assumed, rather than fitted.

This is a simplified sequential assessment for teaching. It does not refit the entire historical time series. The uncertainty range is approximate and conditional on its assumptions; it omits uncertainty in q, biological rates and model structure.

Accounting equations and limits
Revenue = actual catch (t) × 1,000 × price ($/kg).
Segment catch = total catch × allocated share.
Segment cost = fixed cost + variable-cost multiplier × cost per unit F × actual F × allocated share.
Segment job-years = segment catch / tonnes per job-year.
NPV = sum of annual profit / (1 + discount rate)year, starting at year 1.
Annual public survey cost = survey visits × cost per visit / projection years.

Allocation assumes the same selectivity and proportional fishing pressure for both segments. A segment with zero allocation incurs no costs; otherwise its fixed costs remain during closures. F is an effort proxy, not measured vessel effort. Operating profit excludes the public survey cost, which is reported separately. The model omits price responses, capital dynamics, fleet entry/exit, household welfare and distribution within segments. These accounting scenarios do not feed back into the population or HCR. Job-years are a landings-supported proxy, not observed employment.

Objective scores

How are scores calculated?
Stock safety = 100 × (1 − crossing risk).
Profit = 100 × clamp(mean annual profit / profit target, 0, 1).
Livelihoods = 100 × clamp(mean job-years / livelihoods target, 0, 1).
Catch = 100 × clamp(mean catch / catch target, 0, 1).
Stability = 100 × clamp(1 − mean absolute annual catch change / tolerance, 0, 1).
Role score = sum(objective score × objective weight) / sum(weights).
Overall score = sum(role score × influence) / sum(influence).

Scores saturate at 100. Negative profits receive zero profit points. Hard limits are checked separately using the unscaled performance measures. A weighted leader is a discussion aid, not an automatic management recommendation.

    Model equations, assumptions and classroom experiments

    Population model

    Annual numbers at age form the operating model. All three presets use the same equations. Growth and maturity vary by age. Fishing begins at the selected age. Spawning biomass is SSB = Σ Nawama.

    N[a+1,t+1] = N[a,t] exp(−M − F[t] selectivity[a])
    Final age group also retains its own survivors.
    Beverton–Holt mean R = 4 h R₀ S / {SSB₀(1−h) + (5h−1)S}
    History: supplied F[t]; R[t+1] = mean R(SSB[t])
    Projection: HCR determines fishing advice;
    R[t+1] = mean R(SSB[t]) × mean-one lognormal error

    Recruitment enters age 1. Weight follows a cubic von Bertalanffy growth curve. Maturity is logistic. Natural mortality M is instantaneous per year. The final age class is open-ended, not a hard lifespan cap. All stocks scale to 1,000 t unfished SSB.

    Survey observations and assessment

    I[t] = q × Σ survey selectivity[a] × N[a,t] × weight[a] × mean-one lognormal error
    p[a,t] = selectivity[a] N[a,t] / Σ selectivity[a] N[a,t]
    Age counts ~ Multinomial(sample size, p)

    The aggregate index measures survey-selected biomass in relative units. Age composition measures sampled numbers, not biomass. Both products can come from one scheduled survey. Ages are assumed to be read without error; the terminal age is a plus group.

    The default assessment fits log numbers at age to a lognormal index likelihood, an optional multinomial age likelihood and independent normal penalties around its predicted log abundance. Prediction uses assumed biology and previous reported total catch; F is solved with the Baranov equation. An approximate Gaussian information matrix gives a conditional 80% SSB range. Prediction propagates diagonal variances and allows process departures; it discards cross-age covariance between years. Zero index CV uses a small numerical log-variance floor of 10⁻⁶.

    Known growth, maturity, survey and fishing selectivity, stock–recruitment settings and unfished SSB are simplifying assumptions. q and natural mortality can be deliberately misspecified. q is fixed by assumption because a relative index and age proportions alone do not identify absolute scale. The two index shortcuts instead assume an unfished age shape and ignore ages and catch in their estimates.

    Catch rules and F reference point rules

    Fixed catch A, B, C: user-selected tonnes / year
    Proportional: C = U × estimated SSB
    Biomass ramp: C = U × estimated SSB × r
    Constant F target: Ftarget = α × management FMSY
    F-based ramp: Ftarget = α × management FMSY × r
    r = clamp[(estimated SSB / SSB₀ − limit) / (trigger − limit), 0, 1]

    The smoothed catch ramp caps changes between advice renewals. Closure overrides that cap at an assessment; reopening uses raw ramp advice. U is a fraction of estimated spawning biomass, distinct from fishing mortality F. α is the adjustable FMSY multiplier. F targets have the same 3/year capacity ceiling as the operating model.

    F rules convert the target into a quota using the Baranov equation and estimated numbers at age. The fitted assessment supplies its own age estimate. The direct and averaged-survey methods use an assumed unfished age composition scaled to estimated SSB. No true numbers at age enter this calculation.

    How reference points are calculated

    For each candidate constant F, age-specific survivorship gives spawning biomass per recruit. The chosen stock–recruitment relationship determines equilibrium recruitment and age structure; the Baranov equation gives equilibrium catch. A grid search followed by numerical refinement finds the F that maximizes this catch. That rate is FMSY; its catch is MSY and its spawning biomass is SSBMSY. These are deterministic equilibrium benchmarks, without recruitment or implementation noise.

    Management reference points use the assumed mortality multiplier; the operating-model FMSY uses true biology for the teaching plot. The recruitment relationship and steepness are assumed known by the manager in this example. Changing recruitment CV changes simulation risk, but not these deterministic reference points.

    Fishing, timing and scoring

    Surveys follow their own schedule. Assessments start in projection year 1 and repeat at the chosen interval. At each assessment, all survey data since the previous assessment are processed with past reported catch; the HCR sets an annual quota that is held until the next assessment. Between assessments, no new data update the estimate and no emergency HCR override is applied. Implementation error changes intended removals each year. The Baranov catch equation determines the fishing mortality needed to take that catch. Fish then survive and age, and new recruits enter the next year.

    Catch[a] = N[a] × F[a]/(M+F[a]) × {1 − exp(−M−F[a])}
    F[a] = F × selectivity[a]

    Fishing mortality has a capacity ceiling of F = 3 per year, so an impossible quota remains partly unfilled. Risk counts SSB below the true limit at least once in years 1 through the selected horizon. Mean catch averages all years and futures. Catch change is the mean absolute difference between adjacent annual realized catches, in tonnes.

    The true population starts at the unfished age distribution. Each supplied historical F value acts for one year; Baranov catch, survival, aging and expected Beverton–Holt recruitment generate the next state. The final historical age vector becomes projection year 0, with no rescaling to a chosen depletion level. Historical recruitment is deterministic in this demonstration, so all trials share the same true historical population.

    The manager begins history with its assumed unfished population, then uses noisy historical surveys and perfectly reported catches to update its own state. Each replicate has its own historical survey errors. All future rules share that replicate’s conditioned estimator and the same terminal true age vector. Survey scheduling continues across the handoff. The smoothed rule uses the final historical catch as its initial change-cap baseline.

    Classroom experiments

    1. Load unfished history, then increasing fishing. Inspect prescribed F, spawning biomass and historical recruits. How does the history determine the age structure and SSB at the projection start?
    2. Paste your own annual F values. Compare sustained heavy fishing with fishing followed by lower F. Keep biology fixed and explain the different population responses.
    3. Change Beverton–Holt steepness h. Inspect how stock–recruitment, the historical population and calculated reference points respond.
    4. From the same historical population, compare catch A–C and the F rules. Try FMSY multipliers 0.75, 1.00 and 1.25. Distinguish prescribed historical F from future target and realized F.
    5. Increase survey CV and compare the three estimators. Which differences come from the true population, and which come from what the manager observes?

    Model boundaries and sources

    A single combined-sex stock, fixed growth and maturity, independent recruitment deviations, known unfished SSB, no movement, no ageing error, independent multinomial age samples and a simplified economic accounting extension. Economic assumptions and role scores do not alter biological dynamics. The biological limit and trigger are classroom choices. A longer projection horizon gives a different crossing-risk question.
